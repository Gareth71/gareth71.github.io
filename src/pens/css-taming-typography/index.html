Dracula

Bram Stoker — Chapter 1, Jonathan Harker's Journal

3 May. Bishop Stratford.—Left Munich at 8:35 P.M., on 1st May, arriving at Vienna early next morning; should have arrived at 6:46, but train was an hour late. Buda-Pesth seems a wonderful place, from the glimpse which I got of it from the train and the little I could walk through the streets. I feared to go very far from the station, as we had arrived late and would start as near the correct time as possible. The impression I had was that we were leaving the West and entering the East; the most western of splendid bridges over the Danube, which is here of noble width and depth, took us among the traditions of Turkish rule.

The Count's Castle

We left in pretty good time, and came after nightfall to Klausenburgh. Here I stopped for the night at the Hotel Royale. I had for dinner, or rather supper, a chicken done up some way with red pepper, which was very good but thirsty. Mem., get recipe for Mina. I asked the waiter, and he said it was called "paprika hendl," and that, as it was a national dish, I should be able to get it anywhere along the Carpathians. I found my smattering of German very useful here, indeed, I don't know how I should be able to get on without it.

Provisions for the Journey

  • A stout ash walking-stick, tipped with iron
  • Crucifix and rosary, given by the innkeeper's wife at Bistritz 🕯️
  • A small flask of slivovitz against the mountain cold
  • Maps of the Bukovina borderlands, much creased from handling
  • Letters of introduction to the Count, sealed in oilskin

A Glossary of Local Terms

Ordog
Satan, in the tongue of the Wallachian peasants I met upon the road.
Pokol
Hell — a word the old women murmured and crossed themselves after speaking.
Vrolok / Vlkoslak
Slovak and Serbian words for the same terrible thing: something that preys upon the living, be it vampire or werewolf.

The whole affair struck me as difficult, almost official, in its formality — the innkeeper's wife would not accept payment for the crucifix, and grew unaccountably agitated, gesturing toward the darkening hills beyond Bistritz. I confess an inexplicable, superstitious uneasiness settled over me as the horses were harnessed for the last, unlit stretch of road toward the castle. 🦇

"He is a very interesting man in himself. When we sat down to dinner he made a remark on the notes for the tale of his journey to London."

Having had some time at my disposal when in London, I had visited the British Museum, and made search among the books and maps in the library regarding Transylvania; it had struck me that some foreknowledge of the country could hardly fail to have some importance in dealing with a nobleman of that country. I found that the district he named was in the extreme east of the country, just on the borders of three states, Transylvania, Moldavia and Bukovina, in the midst of the Carpathian mountains; one of the wildest and least known portions of Europe.

The district I had noted was the extreme east of Transylvania, and I set myself to search out its history and legends, as much as time would allow. It seems to me that the more I read of superstition and old wives' tales, the more curious my journey becomes; a strange land, a strange people, strange things which the sane mind can hardly credit — and yet I must write it all down, if only that Mina may one day read it and understand what befell her husband on the road to a castle few dare visit and fewer still return from unscathed.
